# Platform Capitalism: Rent, Data, and the New Monopolies

*Six weeks · about 5 hours a week · taught from Abeer Minhas's shelf, assembled by the council*

A six-unit reading course on how digital platforms make money, what they extract, whom they classify, and whether any of it is new. We begin with the mechanics of platform revenue, immediately test the extraction framing against the mainstream economics of multisided markets, then move through the technofeudalism debate, the hidden labor that makes 'automation' work, and the classificatory and surveillance consequences of platform infrastructure. The reading list is deliberately trimmed: each week is roughly 150 pages, and the course names the gaps in its own library rather than pretending to cover them.

## Week 1 — Where the Money Actually Comes From

**By the end of this week you should be able to:**

- Distinguish the main platform business models (advertising, cloud, industrial, product, lean) and specify the revenue mechanism proper to each.
- Argue that data extraction, network effects, and monopolization are structurally linked rather than incidental to one another.
- Use a named mechanism, not a metaphor, whenever claiming a platform 'extracts' value.

**Readings**

- **Platform Capitalism** — Nick Srnicek  
  ~150pp, chapters on platform typology (advertising, cloud, lean platforms) · ~150pp
  > A short critical analysis of how digital platforms became a dominant business model for extracting and monetising data under contemporary capitalism.

**Where the sources disagree**  
This is a single-source week, so the disagreement is internal and prospective: Srnicek's typology is built to show that platforms are an intensification of capitalist competition, not a departure from it — a position that Varoufakis (Unit 3) rejects outright and that Evans and Schmalensee (Unit 2) would say misdescribes the coordination problem platforms solve. Read Srnicek now knowing that his framing of data as extracted raw material is a contested premise, not a neutral description. What a solo week hides: with Larson's firm-by-firm histories cut, you get the model without the ballast of how any specific company's revenue actually evolved.

## Week 2 — The Case for Platforms: Matching Markets and Digital Opportunity

**By the end of this week you should be able to:**

- Explain the two-sided-market account of why platforms exist — chicken-and-egg coordination, cross-side network effects, and asymmetric pricing — in terms a regulator would recognize.
- Argue where 'monopoly' outcomes plausibly reflect winner-take-most network effects rather than rent extraction, and where that defence fails.
- Distinguish the claim that platforms are good for particular users from the claim that the platform business model is economically sound, and say which Omojuwa actually makes.

**Readings**

- **Matchmakers: The New Economics of Multisided Platforms** — David S. Evans and Richard Schmalensee · **not on the shelf**  
  Chapters 1-4 (the core theory of why multisided/two-sided platforms exist, network effects, and pricing logic) — roughly 80-100 pages · ~90pp
- **Digital: The New Code of Wealth** — Joshua J Omojuwa  
  ~150pp, chapters making the core 'digital wealth creation' argument · ~150pp
  > A Nigerian digital strategist argues that digital platforms and skills are the new currency of wealth, with lessons for Africa's economy.

**Where the sources disagree**  
The heaviest week on the list (~240pp), and deliberately so: it is the only place the course hears a defence. Evans and Schmalensee argue from industrial-organization economics that multisided platforms create genuine value by solving matching problems, and that concentration can be an equilibrium property of network effects rather than evidence of predation — the exact framework antitrust authorities apply. Omojuwa argues something different and weaker: that platform access is real opportunity for African entrepreneurs regardless of who owns the pipes. They do not defend the same thing, and reading them together should make the gap obvious. Evans and Schmalensee is not from the professor's shelf; it was added because every other economic reading on this list treats extraction as given.

## Week 3 — Technofeudalism, or Capitalism Wearing a Hoodie

**By the end of this week you should be able to:**

- State Varoufakis's cloud-capital thesis precisely — rent displacing profit, cloudalists and cloud proles — and identify what empirical claim would falsify it.
- Argue for or against the proposition that platform rent is categorically different from monopoly pricing, using Unit 1 and Unit 2 as the test.
- Explain why the definitional fight matters for remedy design, not just for labelling.

**Readings**

- **Technofeudalism: What Killed Capitalism** — Yanis Varoufakis  
  ~150pp, core chapters on rent extraction and platform 'fiefdoms' · ~150pp
  > Varoufakis argues that Big Tech's cloud capital has replaced markets with digital fiefdoms, supplanting capitalism with a new feudal order.

**Where the sources disagree**  
The live three-way fight of the course. Varoufakis: this is no longer capitalism; markets have been replaced by fiefdoms and profit by rent. Srnicek (Unit 1): it is capitalism, intensified — the same competitive dynamics producing the same monopolies, with data as the new input. Evans and Schmalensee (Unit 2): 'rent' is the wrong frame entirely, because the returns to a successful matchmaker are returns to solving a coordination problem competitors failed to solve. Lepore's Simulmatics prehistory, which would have shown that data-driven behavioural prediction long predates cloud infrastructure and thus cuts against the novelty claim, was cut for budget; the novelty argument therefore goes less tested than it should.

## Week 4 — Who Does the Work the Platform Calls Automatic

**By the end of this week you should be able to:**

- Argue that 'automation' in commercial AI systems is a labor-classification achievement as much as a technical one.
- Describe the mechanisms — on-demand contracting, task fragmentation, algorithmic dispatch — that make this workforce invisible in both the product and the balance sheet.
- Connect ghost work back to Unit 1's revenue mechanisms: name whose margin it protects.

**Readings**

- **Ghost Work: How to Stop Silicon Valley from Building a New Global Underclass – An Urgent Investigation into the Invisible Human Labor Powering AI and the Digital Economy** — Mary L. Gray  
  Chapters 1-3 plus concluding chapters, ~150pp · ~150pp
  > An investigation into the hidden global workforce of on-demand contractors who label data and clean up behind automated systems.

**Where the sources disagree**  
These sources mostly agree — because there is only one, and that is the problem. Gray and Suri are broadly sympathetic to the workers and reformist about the fix (portable benefits, better task design), which puts them nearer the Srnicek end than the Varoufakis end: this is exploitation within capitalism, remediable in principle. What one ethnography hides: nothing here on drivers, warehouse workers, content moderators as an occupational category, or platform unionism, and no management-side or economic defence of contractor classification. Liu's founder-side disillusionment, which would have supplied an insider counterpoint, is not on the confirmed list. Read Gray as evidence, not as the labor literature.

## Week 5 — Sorted by Search: Classification as Infrastructure

**By the end of this week you should be able to:**

- Argue that search results are an editorial and commercial product, not a mirror of the world, and identify where the bias enters.
- Explain how advertising-funded ranking (Unit 1) produces racialized and gendered classification as a business outcome rather than a bug.
- Assess the claim that technical fixes to ranking cannot resolve a harm whose source is the revenue model.

**Readings**

- **Algorithms of Oppression: How Search Engines Reinforce Racism** — Safiya Umoja Noble  
  ~150pp, introduction plus core case-study chapters · ~150pp
  > An examination of how commercial search engines encode racist and sexist bias in their results and shape public knowledge.

**Where the sources disagree**  
Noble's argument runs against the industry's own position that ranking merely reflects aggregate user behaviour — the neutrality defence that Broussard names 'technochauvinism.' Broussard's book was cut for overlap with Noble, so the ideology gets critiqued here but not anatomized; you will have to reconstruct the neutrality claim from Noble's account of it, which is by design unsympathetic. Note also the tension with Unit 2: if search is a matchmaker priced asymmetrically to advertisers, Evans and Schmalensee would predict exactly the skew Noble documents without conceding it is racism — that is a disagreement about what the finding means, not whether it is real.

## Week 6 — Borders, Cages, and the Right to Be Unseen

**By the end of this week you should be able to:**

- Argue how biometric and algorithmic systems extend border and carceral control beyond the territorial border, and who bears the error rate.
- Distinguish abolitionist remedies from reformist ones and state the strongest objection to each.
- Defend or reject the claim that anonymity is a political good and not merely a private preference.

**Readings**

- **Resisting Borders and Technologies of Violence (Abolitionist Papers)** — Ruha Benjamin  
  Selected essays on border surveillance and carceral tech (approx. 100-120 pages) · ~110pp
  > An anthology examining how surveillance technologies enforce borders and how movements resist carceral and migration control systems.
- **How to Disappear: Notes on Invisibility in a Time of Transparency** — Akiko Busch  
  ~40pp, one or two central essays on visibility/anonymity · ~40pp
  > Essays exploring the value of anonymity and reticence in an age of constant visibility, drawing on nature, art, and technology.

**Where the sources disagree**  
A genuine mismatch of register and of politics. The Benjamin anthology treats surveillance as state and corporate violence with collective, abolitionist remedies — dismantle the system, not tune it. Busch treats invisibility as an individual ethical and aesthetic good, recoverable through reticence, with almost no account of who is denied the option of being unseen. Read against each other, Busch's essays are either the phenomenology the abolitionist case needs or a privileged misreading of what surveillance costs; say which and defend it. Eggers's fiction, which would have supplied the internalized, self-policing version of the same experience, was cut.

## What this syllabus doesn't cover

Six units were written, but two of the six original threads are not covered and one is covered thinly. There is no legal-doctrine unit: the thread on remedy (merger review, essential facilities, consumer welfare, interoperability mandates) had no confirmed readings — Doctorow, McNamee, and Wynn-Williams sit on the shelf but did not survive verification into the confirmed list, and the library contains no legal scholarship at all. Students will therefore leave able to describe the harms and the economics but not the doctrine that would have to be moved. The pro-platform thread survives only as Unit 2, and only because a non-shelf book (Evans and Schmalensee) was added; with The Every cut there is no reading on users who want the surveillance. Labor coverage is one ethnography (Gray): nothing on drivers, warehouses, content moderation as an industry, or platform unions. Also absent by cut or by library: firm-level histories (Larson), the prehistory of data prediction (Lepore), the anatomy of technochauvinism (Broussard), all fiction, platform cooperatives and public alternatives, and any non-US platform ecosystem apart from Omojuwa's African entrepreneurship case.

## The counter-reader's objection

Every reading on this syllabus that addresses 'where the money comes from' or 'is this a new order' treats platform economics as extractive by construction — rent, data-harvesting, monopoly, feudal enclosure — whether from Srnicek's Marxian typology, Varoufakis's technofeudalism thesis, or Larson's left political economy. The one dissenting voice (Omojuwa) argues platforms are good for individual users/entrepreneurs, not that the platform business model itself is economically sound. What is entirely absent is the mainstream economics account of why multi-sided platforms exist and often lower costs or solve real coordination problems — the two-sided-market theory that underpins actual antitrust and competition-policy debate about these firms. Without it, students only ever see platforms indicted, never modeled by the discipline (industrial-organization economics) that regulators and courts actually use to evaluate them, so they can't tell where the political-economy critiques are empirically strong versus where they're arguing against an economic logic they haven't been shown.
